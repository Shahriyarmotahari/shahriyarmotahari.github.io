Research focus

Breaking the unbreakable

PFAS such as PFOA are held together by carbon–fluorine bonds (≈485 kJ/mol), among the strongest in organic chemistry. That is why they persist in water and resist conventional treatment. My PhD work engineers electrochemical reactors that break those bonds and mineralize the molecule.

PFOA · C8HF15O2

About

I’m a PhD candidate in Chemical Engineering at the University of British Columbia, which I joined in 2024, where I research the electrochemical treatment of per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) in water. I design and optimize reactors and electrodes that degrade these persistent contaminants more completely and efficiently. I also study how real water chemistry, such as natural organic matter and electrolyte concentration, affects removal, selectivity and energy demand over long-term operation.

I earned my BSc in 2018 and my MSc in 2022, both in Chemical Engineering at Sharif University of Technology, where I built a fungal bioreactor that removes pharmaceuticals from water. Between the MSc and the PhD I worked in industry: as an R&D engineer on atmospheric water harvesting, and in business and research development on filtration systems and EPC projects.

Alongside research, I teach Introduction to Engineering with UBC Extended Learning, serve as a teaching assistant in water, environmental and chemical engineering courses, and mentor undergraduate researchers in the lab.

Research

Engineering treatment processes for contaminants that conventional systems leave behind.

UBC · Chemical & Biological Engineering 2024–present

A novel electrochemical system that degrades and mineralizes PFAS far more completely than a conventional cell, while using roughly a third less energy.

+30–40%degradation and mineralization
≈ −30%energy consumption

This research investigates electrochemical oxidation for the degradation of both short- and long-chain per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) in contaminated water. The work examines how different PFAS compounds respond to treatment, focusing on degradation efficiency, energy consumption and the formation of transformation products. It also evaluates defluorination — the release of fluorine from PFAS molecules as fluoride — to better understand the extent of their breakdown. The overall goal is to deepen our understanding of electrochemical PFAS destruction and support the development of effective, energy-efficient treatment technologies for these persistent environmental contaminants.

  • Optimized reactor performance across hydrodynamic, electrochemical and electrode-design parameters: flow rate, current density, electrode spacing and particle loading.
  • Assessed how natural organic matter and electrolyte concentration affect PFAS removal, selectivity and energy demand under environmentally relevant conditions.
  • Investigated long-term operation, electrode passivation and in-situ regeneration strategies to reduce downtime.
  • Supervise and mentor undergraduate researchers in experimental planning, lab methods and data analysis.
Sharif University · MSc 2019–2022

A rotating biological contactor carrying an immobilized Trametes biofilm, which combines fungal biodegradation with advanced bio-oxidation to remove pharmaceuticals from wastewater.

100%diclofenac removal
≈ 60%carbamazepine removal
+30%performance vs. the control system

This project investigated the removal of pharmaceutical contaminants from wastewater using a fungal rotating biological contactor (RBC). Trametes fungi were immobilized on carriers, and the rotation of the drum promoted contact between the fungal biomass, the wastewater and the air. The work compared conventional biological treatment with advanced bio-oxidation for the removal of diclofenac, metronidazole and levofloxacin. Under the laboratory conditions tested, the combined approach achieved complete removal of diclofenac and approximately 60% removal of carbamazepine, which points to the potential of fungal systems for treating pharmaceutical wastewater.

  • Engineered the biofilm to generate hydrogen peroxide in situ, pairing fungal biodegradation with biologically mediated advanced oxidation.
  • Separated biodegradation from adsorption and traced degradation pathways and transformation intermediates.
Bonda Innovation Center 2021–2022

An electrically assisted fog-harvesting system that recovers water from airborne droplets, using a high voltage to pull them onto a mesh collector.

≈ 25%water-collection efficiency of the prototype
3×collection efficiency with the high-voltage assist

As part of the Bonda group, I worked on developing an electrically assisted fog-harvesting system to recover water from airborne droplets. Our laboratory setup combined a fog generator, a high-voltage power supply and a mesh collector positioned above the fog chamber to promote electrostatic droplet capture. The prototype achieved approximately 25% water-collection efficiency under the conditions tested. The work explored an approach with potential applications in atmospheric water harvesting and in water recovery from industrial cooling-tower plumes.

  • The high-voltage assist tripled collection efficiency compared with the unassisted collector.
  • Modified the surface of the collector to improve droplet capture and overall system performance.
